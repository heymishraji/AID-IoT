How to judge a typeface, beyond how it looks

Looks matter, but a brand font spends most of its life in tables, labels, datasheets and small print, not in headlines. These six questions, weighted for AID, decide whether it holds up there. Devanagari is left out on purpose: it will get its own font later. The weights feed the scorecard at the end.

Matters less than it seems

How the font looks in the logo (a wordmark is usually drawn or adjusted by hand, not typed). How pretty the specimen alphabet is in isolation. What is fashionable this year: the font has to outlast the trend.

The process

  1. Longlist by job18 candidates across six styles, already checked for basics. This page.

Claude's read

    

      
Your instinct holds up. Atkinson Hyperlegible Next was built so that easily confused letters look different, and the file checks confirm it has what AID needs every day: weights from 200 to 800, true italics, tabular figures, the rupee sign, and its own monospace partner for device IDs and coordinates. Few families have all of these.

      
Its one real gap: no narrow width for dense tables, so Lighthouse and datasheet tables have to use size and weight instead. Devanagari is not needed now and can get its own font later. Its distinctive letter shapes give it character; check that headlines feel confident enough for AID IoT and calm enough for AID Defence.

      
Strongest challengers, each better at one thing:

      
    
        
  • Fira Sans: the closest all-rounder. Built for small screens, condensed families for tables, Fira Code and Fira Mono, italics. Widely used in tech since 2014, so less distinctive.
    • 
        
  • Archivo: one industrial family from tight datasheet tables (width 62) to wide headlines (125). Less warmth in running text.
    • 
        
  • Source Sans 3: the calmest humanist workhorse, digits aligned by default, Source Code Pro as its mono. Quiet to the point of anonymous.
    • 
      

      
Flagged by the checks: Lexend and Inclusive Sans cannot align digits in columns; Barlow and Instrument Sans have no rupee sign. They stay on the board so you can see the trade-off, but they would need workarounds.
